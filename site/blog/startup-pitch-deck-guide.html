Most first decks try to say everything. The best ones say one thing clearly: here is a real problem, here is why we're the team to solve it now, and here is the proof. Everything else is supporting evidence.

What a deck is really for

A pitch deck is not a contract or a full business plan, it's a door-opener. Its only job is to make a busy investor curious enough to take the meeting. That means clarity beats completeness: one idea per slide, plain language, and numbers that matter rather than numbers that fill space. If a slide doesn't move the story forward, it's costing you attention.

The 11 slides investors expect

Order varies, but a strong seed deck almost always covers these, roughly one slide each:

  • Problem, the pain, made concrete and human.
  • Solution, what you built, in one sentence anyone understands.
  • Why now, the shift that makes this the right moment.
  • Market, who has this problem and how many of them there are.
  • Product, show it working, not just describe it.
  • Moat, why this is hard to copy: IP, data, hardware, distribution.
  • Traction, evidence it's real: users, revenue, pilots, partnerships.
  • Competition, an honest landscape and your wedge.
  • Business model, how money is made and at what margin.
  • Team, why you specifically will win.
  • The ask, how much you're raising and what it will pay for.

How we framed Vahini's deck

For Vahini, a deeptech hardware startup, the story leans on three of those slides especially hard. The why-now is a genuine crisis: rising screen time is eroding the hand-brain coordination handwriting builds, and educators are sounding the alarm from kindergarten to grade 12. The moat is concrete: a granted Indian patent, a full in-house stack (we mould the mechanics, write the firmware, build the AI), and an Indic handwriting-motion dataset across five scripts that Western pens can't serve.

And the competition slide is where a clear wedge shows: rival pens either need special dot-pattern paper or skip Indic scripts and coaching entirely. Framing those honestly, rather than claiming we have no competition, is what makes the rest of the deck credible. You can see exactly how we laid this out on our investors page.

A deck doesn't need to prove you'll win. It needs to make a smart person want to find out.

Key takeaways

  • A deck's only job is to earn the next meeting, clarity beats completeness.
  • Cover the 11 expected slides, one idea each, with numbers that matter.
  • For deeptech, lean hardest on why-now, moat and an honest competition slide.
  • Start from a working template, then make the story unmistakably yours.
